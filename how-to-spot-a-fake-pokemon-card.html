How to spot a fake Pokemon card

Nine checks, ordered by how many counterfeits each one catches. None of them need a loupe, a scale or a grading slab - and the first three take under a minute on a card you are holding.

If you read only one thing

Check the back of the card, then the energy symbols, then the printed number against the set's real card list. Counterfeiters reproduce the front artwork well and the small printed details badly. A seller who will not show you the back is telling you something.

1Start with the card back

The back is a single shared print design across an entire era of the game, which makes it the easiest thing to compare against a known genuine card. Fake backs are frequently the wrong shade, faded, off-centre, or have a border that does not match. If you own any recent genuine Pokemon card, put it next to the suspect one in the same light and compare the two backs directly.

2Look at the symbols, not the artwork

The small printed icons are where low-quality counterfeits fall apart. On fake cards, energy symbols and other printed icons are often misshapen, incorrectly sized, misaligned with the surrounding text, or have the inner icon printed off-centre. Check these against a real card of the same era rather than trusting the picture on the front.

3Read the text at arm's length and then close up

  • Genuine cards print with sharp, evenly weighted text and consistent letter spacing.
  • Fakes commonly show blurry or soft characters, uneven spacing, colours that are too light or too dark, and HP or damage numbers that look wrong for the format.
  • An HP value far outside what the format supports is a fast tell - the number is printed from a template the counterfeiter did not understand.

4Holo, foil and texture

Holographic cards have a raised, structured print layer. A flat rainbow sheen printed onto ordinary card stock is the classic counterfeit holo: it glitters in a photo but has no texture under a fingernail and no consistent dot or line pattern when you tilt it against a light. Rarity symbols and the frame finish should match other cards from the same set.

5Card stock, edges and size

Real Pokemon cards are cut cleanly to a standard size with a firm, layered card stock feel. Counterfeits are often thinner, softer, waxy, slightly oversize or undersize, or have edges that feel rough or visibly layered apart. Compare the suspect card edge-to-edge with a genuine one - a size difference of even a millimetre is obvious when two cards are laid together.

6The collector number has to exist

Every card prints position / set size. The number is only meaningful with the set logo beside it, and the test is whether that exact entry appears in that set's official card list. This is where two widespread myths do the most damage:

Myth: "a number higher than the set size is fake." Genuine secret rares are printed above the set total on purpose - Pokemon Card 151 runs to 165 and prints its secret rares from 166/165 up to 207/165. Rejecting anything above the total rejects some of the most valuable cards in the hobby.

Myth: "a plausible number means the card is fine." The counterfeit Pikachu ex printed 132/106 with a Super Electric Breaker set name is recorded in the ungraded.com Fake Card Database. It is the number existing at all that matters, not the arithmetic.

7The 30-second check: the official app

The free official Pokemon TCG mobile app has a scan feature. Point it at the physical card and confirm it recognises the same name, set and collector number the seller claims. This is a real lookup against the official database, and it is the single best check a buyer can do without equipment. It is not proof - a scan of a good reference image can be faked, and a listing photo is not the card - but a seller who cannot or will not produce a scan result is a seller taking no responsibility for their listing.

8Sealed product is harder, not easier

Counterfeit packs, boxes and cases are common, and you cannot inspect the contents. Check foil quality and crimp pattern, print sharpness on the box, and whether the seller photographs the case, the box and the packs together rather than a single stock-looking shot. A price that only makes sense if the box is full of chase cards is the tell.

Do not do these

Skip the destructive "community tests". Bending, scratching, tearing or comparing the inner layers of a card can destroy a genuine card, ruin its grade and cost you more than the counterfeit you were trying to avoid. Non-destructive checks come first for a reason.

Already bought one?

  1. Photograph the card, its back, the packaging and the listing as it still appears - do this before the seller edits the listing.
  2. Open the dispute with the marketplace or payment provider you used, in that order. A card-issuer or e-wallet chargeback claim is usually faster than any marketplace case, and it does not depend on the seller cooperating.
  3. Keep the item. Destroying or returning it without a written agreement removes your evidence.

Frequently asked questions

Can you tell a fake Pokemon card from a photo?

Partly. Print blur, misaligned symbols and a wrong back can show in a good photo; stock, thickness, size and holo texture cannot. Front-only listings are the most common warning sign because they hide exactly the checks that matter.

Do Pokemon error cards exist?

Genuine production misprints are rare and are mostly caught and destroyed. In practice the overwhelming majority of cards sold as errors are cut-and-paste fakes or deliberate bootlegs, so treat "error" as a warning sign, not a bargain.

Is the bend test or the tear test safe?

No. Both destroy value and can ruin a genuine card. Non-destructive checks - back, symbols, print sharpness, holo, edges, number, app scan - catch counterfeits without damaging anything.

What is the fastest reliable check?

The official Pokemon TCG app scan against the physical card, confirming the same name, set and collector number the listing claims. Free, and takes about 30 seconds.

Are fake sealed packs common?

Yes, and they are harder to judge than single cards because you cannot see inside. Buy sealed only from sellers who photograph the case, box and pack foil in one set.
